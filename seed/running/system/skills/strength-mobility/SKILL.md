# Strength and mobility

Two short sessions a week of well-chosen strength work is one of the best-supported additions to running. It is also one of the easiest to overdo: sore legs wreck key runs. Keep it small, progressive and boring.

## Why
- **Running economy and performance:** heavy and explosive strength work and plyometrics improve running economy and time-trial performance in distance runners, without adding much body mass (Blagrove et al., 2018; Balsalobre-Fernández et al., 2016). *Moderate evidence.*
- **Injury resilience:** strength training reduces sports injuries overall, with the largest effect on overuse injuries (Lauersen et al., 2014; not running-specific). *Moderate.*
- **Rehab and robustness:** tendons and bones respond to progressive loading; heavy slow resistance is part of standard care for tendinopathies (e.g., Beyer et al., 2015, in the Achilles). *Moderate.*
- It also helps older athletes, who lose muscle and power with age.
Frame it for athletes as "making your running more durable and efficient", never as body shaping.

## The minimum effective dose
**Two sessions a week, 20 to 30 minutes**, all year, trimmed (not dropped) in the taper. Pick 6 to 8 movements across these patterns:
- **Single-leg strength:** split squat, step-up, single-leg Romanian deadlift (RDL), lunge.
- **Calf and Achilles:** straight-knee and bent-knee calf raises, slow and loaded (single leg when strong).
- **Hip and glute:** hip thrust or bridge, side plank or side-lying leg raise, lateral band walk.
- **Posterior chain:** deadlift/RDL variants, hamstring bridge or curl.
- **Trunk:** front and side planks, Pallof press, dead bug (anti-extension, anti-rotation).
- **Plyometrics (once a base exists):** skipping, pogo hops, low box jumps, hill bounds. 2 sets of 6 to 10 contacts to start.
**Dose:** 2 to 3 sets of 3 to 8 heavy reps (experienced, with a spotter or good technique) or 8 to 12 moderate reps (beginners). Stop 1 to 3 reps short of failure. Rest 1 to 2 minutes. Tempo controlled.

**Progression rule:** when all sets hit the top of the rep range with 1 to 2 reps still in reserve for two sessions in a row, add a little load or make the movement harder (more range, single-leg, slower). Add one change at a time. Beginners start with bodyweight and learn the movements for 2 to 4 weeks.

### Sample routine A (no equipment, ~20 min)
Split squat 3 × 8 to 10 per side; single-leg calf raise (off a step) 3 × 10 to 15 per side; glute bridge 3 × 12 (single-leg when easy); side plank 3 × 20 to 40 s per side; single-leg RDL (bodyweight) 3 × 8 per side; dead bug 2 × 8 per side.
### Sample routine B (gym, ~30 min)
Trap-bar or goblet deadlift 3 × 5; Bulgarian split squat 3 × 6 to 8 per side; standing and seated calf raises 3 × 8 to 12; hip thrust 3 × 8; Pallof press 3 × 10 per side; optional pogo hops 2 × 8.
Both: 5 minutes of easy warm-up first (walking, leg swings, a light set of the first movement).

## Scheduling
- Put strength on **easy-run days** or after the run on hard days; keep **hard days hard and easy days easy** together. Leave **at least ~24 hours**, ideally more, between heavy leg work and a key session or the long run. A common pattern: Tuesday (after an easy run) and Friday (before Saturday's short easy run).
- **Taper:** drop to one maintenance session in the week before the race, keep loads, no new movements in the final 7 to 10 days, none in the last 2 to 3 days.
- **Block starts and returns:** introduce strength in a base phase or during a down week, not at peak volume. Soreness (DOMS) is expected after the first sessions; it should be mild by 48 hours; reduce the volume if it isn't.
- After a layoff or injury: return to strength before volume; progress gradually.

## Mobility and flexibility
- A short **dynamic warm-up** (leg swings, marching, skips, strides) before intensity beats long static stretching before running (static stretching in large doses before a run can briefly reduce force).
- Stretching has **not** been shown to prevent injuries (Lauersen et al., 2014). Do it if it feels good or addresses a real limitation (ankle dorsiflexion for squatting, hip extension for stride).
- **Foam rolling**: short-term range-of-motion and soreness benefits, little effect on performance or injury (Wiewelhove et al., 2019); fine for comfort, not a treatment for pain.
- Yoga or Pilates: fine for variety and body awareness; count as strength only if loaded enough.

## Pain and modification
- Muscle soreness: fine. **Sharp joint pain, pain during a movement that persists after, or tendon pain >3/10:** modify the movement (range, load, tempo), swap it, or stop and apply `injury-and-pain`.
- Red flags (§11) apply here too (chest pain, dizziness on exertion).
- Not appropriate without clinician input: pregnancy or postpartum return, recent surgery, osteoporosis or known bone issues, significant cardiac or blood-pressure conditions. Ask first, then follow their guidance. Don't lift heavy with poor technique: suggest a coach or video review if unsure.

## Presenting and logging
Add as `planned_workouts` of type `strength` with a short `description` ("Strength A, 25 min") and a `structure` if useful; mark the session in `activities` with sport `strength`, duration and RPE (it counts in session-RPE load, `training-load`). Show a one-page routine in chat (an image or a list of five lines) and offer a short video link only if the athlete asks (don't invent links).

## Worked example
A 4-day-a-week half-marathon runner (45 km/week) has never lifted. Weeks 1 to 2: routine A bodyweight, Tuesday after an easy run and Friday, with soreness check. Weeks 3 to 6: add a backpack or dumbbells to split squats and RDLs; calf raises to single-leg. Week 7: a down week: halve sets, keep loads. Weeks 8 to 10: routine B-like loads if gym access appeared, 3 × 5 on deadlift/split squat; add 2 sets of pogo hops. Taper (week 11): Tuesday only, loads unchanged, 2 sets. They log RPE; you watch the Thursday tempo quality for signs the heavy session is leaking into key runs, and adjust.

## Pitfalls
Too much too soon. Heavy legs the day before a long run or race. Bodybuilding-style splits. Treating "core" as the main thing. Boring programs that lead to dropout: keep two sessions easy to start. Using foam rolling or stretching to manage pain that needs assessment. Body-composition framing, especially for athletes at risk of disordered eating.

## Evidence notes (citations from memory; verify before quoting)
- Economy and performance: *moderate* (Blagrove et al., Sports Med 2018; Balsalobre-Fernández et al., J Strength Cond Res 2016).
- Overuse injury reduction: *moderate* (Lauersen et al., Br J Sports Med 2014), mostly non-running and mixed populations.
- Heavy slow resistance for Achilles tendinopathy: *moderate* (Beyer et al., Am J Sports Med 2015).
- Stretching for injury prevention: *weak/no benefit*; foam rolling: *weak to moderate* for short-term soreness and range of motion (Wiewelhove et al., Front Physiol 2019).
- Frequency, sets and reps, taper handling: *expert opinion and extrapolation* from strength-training research.

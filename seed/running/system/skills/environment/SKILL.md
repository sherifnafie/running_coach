# Environment: heat, cold, altitude, air quality

The environment changes what a given effort produces, and in extremes it changes what is safe. The rule that covers most cases: **hold effort, not pace**, and when conditions are dangerous, change the session, not the athlete's resolve.

## Using the forecast
Check the forecast (temperature, **dew point or humidity**, wind, air quality) before a key session, using a scheduled wake the evening before or the morning of. City-level weather searches are fine; don't include names, street addresses or health details in a query. Offer options rather than orders: shift the time, shorten, move indoors, swap days.

## Heat and humidity
**Why it matters.** Running generates heat; the body cools mainly by sweating, which works poorly in humid air. Heart rate climbs, pace falls for the same effort, and the risk of exertional heat illness rises. Dew point is a better guide to comfort than temperature alone (roughly: under 10 °C / 50 °F pleasant, 15 to 18 °C (60 to 65 °F) sticky, 20 °C+ (68 °F+) oppressive).

**Adjusting pace, as a rough guide** (the popular "temperature + dew point" heuristic: *weak evidence, widely used*). Add the air temperature and dew point in °F: ≤ 100 no adjustment; 101 to 110 ~0 to 0.5% slower; 111 to 120 ~0.5 to 1%; 121 to 130 ~1 to 2%; 131 to 140 ~2 to 3%; 141 to 150 ~3 to 4.5%; 151 to 160 ~4.5 to 6%; 161 to 170 ~6 to 8%; 171 to 180 ~8 to 10%; above 180, hard running or racing isn't advisable. Example: 32 °C (90 °F) with a 22 °C (72 °F) dew point sums to 162: expect ~6 to 8% slower, so a 5:30/km easy run becomes ~5:50 to 5:55. Slower and heavier runners slow more. **Use effort and the talk test as the real guide**; the number only sets expectations.

**Practical steps.**
- Run early or late, in shade, on loops near water or shops; wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing; hat or visor; sunscreen.
- **Drink to thirst** before, during and after. For runs over ~60 to 90 minutes in heat, carry fluid or plan a route with refills; include some salt/electrolytes in long hot sessions. Don't drink more than thirst dictates (overhydration risk; Hew-Butler et al., 2015). Check hydration by thirst and pale urine. **Don't advise weigh-ins** (a research tool that can feed unhealthy body-weight focus).
- Cool down gradually, cool shower, fluids and food afterwards.
- For a hot race: plan a slower start, shade, sponges and ice at aid stations, pre-cooling (cold drinks, cool shower before the start); set a heat-adjusted A goal and a finishing C goal.
- Treadmill with a fan is a legitimate substitute for the hottest days.

**Acclimatization.** About 10 to 14 days of gradual, repeated heat exposure (easier first days, ~60 to 90 minutes of exercise in heat on most days, don't skip) improves sweating, plasma volume and heart-rate response; it fades over ~1 to 2 weeks without exposure (Périard et al., 2015). Traveling to a hot race: arrive early if possible, or use post-run hot baths or sauna for several weeks beforehand (*moderate*, small trials).

**Warning signs and action.** Headache, nausea, dizziness, cramps, unusual fatigue, goosebumps or chills in heat (early heat exhaustion): **stop, get to shade, cool and drink, don't continue**. **Confusion, stumbling, collapse, hot dry skin or no sweating, vomiting, seizure** (possible heat stroke): this is an emergency. Call emergency services and cool them aggressively now (cold water, ice on the neck, armpits and groin; cold-water immersion is the gold standard: "cool first, transport second", Casa et al., 2015). Follow constitution §11.

**Event risk categories.** The ACSM road-race flags use wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT): below ~18 °C low risk; 18 to 23 moderate; 23 to 28 high; above 28 extreme (Armstrong et al., 2007). Races at the high end of this scale need an adjusted plan; at the extreme, skip or convert.

## Cold
- **Clothing:** dress for ~10 °C (15 to 20 °F) warmer than the air once you're running; layers you can shed; wicking base layer; gloves, hat or ear band; windproof outer layer. The first minutes should feel slightly cool.
- **Wind:** start into the wind and finish with it at your back; sweat plus wind chills fast. Change out of wet clothes soon after.
- **Surface and light:** ice and black ice are a bigger danger than the cold: slow down, shorten stride, use traction devices, or go indoors. Short days: reflective gear, a light, known routes.
- **Frostbite and hypothermia:** exposed skin can freeze in about half an hour at very low wind chills (roughly −25 °C / −13 °F and below). Numb, waxy, pale skin on fingers, toes, nose or ears: get warm now. Shivering that won't stop, confusion, slurred speech: hypothermia, an emergency.
- **Cold air and breathing:** it can provoke cough or wheeze, especially with asthma. A longer warm-up, a buff over the mouth, and easier effort help. Persistent wheeze or chest tightness: stop; see a clinician.
- Cold rarely harms performance for steady running; slippery footing and clothing mistakes are the real issues.

## Altitude
- **Effect:** less oxygen in the air means a lower aerobic ceiling: VO2max falls roughly 6 to 8% per 1,000 m above sea level (from about 1,000 to 1,500 m upward; Wehrlin and Hallén, 2006), and race performance falls similarly. The same effort is slower; heart rate is higher at a given pace.
- **Adjust by effort and HR**, expect breathlessness at easy pace; treat the first days as easy. For a trip: shorten and slow the first 3 to 5 days, increase intensity after about a week, expect full adaptation in 2 to 3 weeks (less complete at high altitude).
- **Race at altitude:** arrive either within about 24 hours or after 10 to 14 days; the in-between days can be worse (*expert opinion, weak*). Sleep and hydration suffer; plan for both.
- **Altitude sickness** (typically above ~2,500 m): headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue, poor sleep. Mild: rest, don't go higher, fluids. **Danger signs:** confusion, unsteady walking, breathlessness at rest, wet cough, blue lips: descend and seek urgent medical care; treat as an emergency (§11).
- "Live high, train low" benefits at the elite level are real but impractical for most recreational athletes; don't promise gains from it.

## Air quality
Exercise increases how much polluted air you breathe, so the dose rises. Use a current local index (US AQI, or the local equivalent) at the time of the run, not a daily average. A practical guide (based on public-health guidance such as the US EPA's):
| AQI | Guidance |
|---|---|
| 0 to 50 | Normal. |
| 51 to 100 | Fine for most; those with asthma, heart or lung conditions: watch for symptoms. |
| 101 to 150 | Sensitive people (asthma, heart or lung disease, pregnancy, children, older adults) should reduce prolonged or hard exercise; others: consider easier or shorter runs, avoid intensity. |
| 151 to 200 | Everyone: move indoors or shorten and slow substantially; skip hard sessions. |
| 201+ | Skip outdoor exercise; indoor options only. |
Wildfire smoke, traffic and afternoon ozone are the common culprits; mornings in the countryside tend to be best for ozone. Particulate masks (N95-type) help against smoke but make breathing effortful: not a licence to do hard sessions. Cough, wheeze, chest tightness, burning eyes or throat: stop and go inside; persistent symptoms: see a clinician. For athletes with asthma or heart disease, advise discussing air-quality limits with their doctor.

## Adjusting the plan
Shift easy runs earlier or later, swap the hard session to a better day, move intensity indoors, convert an interval workout into an effort-based fartlek on a hot day, trim the long run's length (not its easiness) in extreme heat, and tell the athlete you're doing it deliberately ("the plan didn't change, the weather did"). Log conditions in `activities.extra` (`temp_c`, `dew_point_c`, `aqi`) so later pace comparisons make sense.

## Pitfalls
Chasing pace in heat. Ignoring dew point. Overdrinking. Recommending weigh-ins. Using a daily-average AQI. Treating a hot-weather bad run as lost fitness. Pushing through early heat illness symptoms. Neglecting ice. Promising altitude benefits.

## Evidence notes (citations from memory; verify before quoting)
- Heat reduces endurance performance, with larger slowing in slower runners and at higher WBGT: *strong* (e.g. Ely et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007).
- Heat acclimatization over ~10 to 14 days: *strong* (Périard et al., Scand J Med Sci Sports 2015).
- Heat-illness recognition and cold-water immersion: *strong consensus* (Casa et al., J Athl Train 2015; Armstrong et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007).
- Temperature-plus-dew-point pace table: *weak* (a popular coaching heuristic; use as a rough guide only).
- Altitude: VO2max decline per 1,000 m *moderate/strong* (Wehrlin and Hallén, Eur J Appl Physiol 2006); arrival-timing advice: *weak/expert opinion*.
- Hydration by thirst: *moderate/consensus* (Hew-Butler et al., Clin J Sport Med 2015).
- Air quality thresholds: *consensus/policy-based* guidance, not individual-level cutoffs.

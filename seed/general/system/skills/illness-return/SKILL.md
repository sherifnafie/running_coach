# Illness and return

Illness is part of training. Two or three days off lose almost no fitness; training through a systemic illness can turn a three-day cold into a three-week slump, and in rare cases it harms the heart. Make resting early feel like the smart decision it is, and drop any guilt.

## 1. Triage
Ask what you need to place it, not a checklist: usually which symptoms and whether there's a fever is enough. Energy, eating and drinking, and resting heart rate matter for anything more than a mild cold.

**The "neck check"** (expert opinion, a long-standing sports-medicine heuristic):
- **Above the neck only** (runny or stuffy nose, sneezing, mild sore throat) and otherwise well: a short, easy session (20 to 30 minutes of conversational cardio, or a light technique or lifting session far from failure; no intensity) is reasonable *if they want to and feel okay*; stop if it feels worse. Skipping is also fine.
- **Below the neck** (chest congestion or tightness, hacking cough, body aches, swollen glands, nausea or diarrhoea, marked fatigue) **or any fever:** no training. Rest.

**Fever rule:** no exercise with a fever (≥ ~38.0 °C / 100.4 °F) or body-wide aches; wait until fever-free for at least 24 hours without fever-reducing medication (expert consensus; some advise 48 hours). The reason: exercise during systemic viral illness raises the (small) risk of heart inflammation and prolongs recovery.
**Dehydration or stomach bug:** no training until eating and drinking normally for a day.

## 2. Red flags during or after illness
Chest pain or pressure, palpitations, unusual breathlessness at rest or with little effort, fainting or near-fainting, a resting heart rate that stays much higher than normal, new severe fatigue out of proportion, confusion, can't keep fluids down for a day, a fever lasting more than ~3 days, or symptoms that worsen after initially improving: stop all exercise and advise medical evaluation (emergency services if severe or happening now). Per §11: don't diagnose; you can say what you're hearing and what to do.

## 3. Return progression
Resume only when symptoms are gone (or just above-the-neck leftovers), no fever for 24 hours, energy mostly back, and a walk feels normal.
| Illness | Out | Return |
|---|---|---|
| Mild, 1 to 3 days, no fever | 1 to 3 days off | Easy session 20 to 30 min next day; ~60 to 70% of normal volume for 2 to 3 days; add intensity after 3 to 4 symptom-free days. |
| Moderate, 4 to 7 days, or fever | until fever-free 24 h plus a recovery day | Easy only for the first week at 50 to 60% of normal volume (or run/walk); no intensity for ~1 week; rebuild over 1 to 2 weeks. |
| Severe, >1 to 2 weeks, hospital, or lingering fatigue | until a clinician agrees | Treat as a gentle restart (run/walk or light circuits, build duration before intensity or load), 3+ weeks; get clinician advice, especially if there were cardiac or respiratory symptoms. |
| COVID-19 or similar | published return-to-play guidance suggests at least ~7 days after symptoms resolve before a staged return, longer if significant illness; clinician review for any chest symptoms | Staged: walking/easy movement, then easy sessions, then volume, then intensity, each stage ≥ 1 to 2 days; stop and seek care for any red flag. |
Use measurable checks: resting HR the morning you return (within ~5 bpm of normal?); HR at an easy effort (a persistent rise of 5 to 10 bpm over normal suggests incomplete recovery); how the first easy session *feels* (RPE up for the same pace or load = wait a day or two). If a session goes badly, take another day or two off; one bad session isn't a setback, ignoring it is. Lifting: return at ~70 to 80% of previous loads with fewer sets and more reps in reserve, then rebuild over about the same number of days you were out; strength comes back faster than endurance.

## 4. Adjust the plan
- **Don't make up missed sessions.** Drop intensity first. Rejoin the plan at the next easy day, at reduced volume, then ramp back over the same number of days you were out (roughly).
- Cut the longest or heaviest session roughly in half the first week back. Reintroduce intensity gradually: for runners strides, then short tempo, then intervals; for lifters moderate sets before heavy singles; for team sports, training before matches.
- Update `plan/current-week.md`, `planned_workouts`, the calendar export and your wakes; tell the athlete what moved and why.
- Fitness: aerobic fitness declines slowly over short breaks (Mujika and Padilla, 2000); a 1 to 2 week break costs little and tends to return within days to a few weeks.
- If illness is **recurrent** (3+ a season), look at load, sleep, stress and energy intake (RED-S, `fueling-basics`) and suggest a doctor.

## 5. Illness near an event
| Timing / severity | Advice |
|---|---|
| Above-the-neck cold, event in 3+ days | Rest easy, keep routine; likely compete, maybe more conservatively. |
| Cold the day before or morning of | Only if symptoms are above the neck, no fever, feels clearly OK on a short easy warm-up; set goals down; "compete by feel". |
| Below the neck, fever, or any red flag | Don't compete. Say so plainly and kindly; offer a later event, or something easy for fun once recovered. |
| Illness during taper | Rest, don't try to "catch up"; the taper volume already protects them. Trust the training they banked. |
Be honest about disappointment; the athlete decides, but you give them a clear recommendation.

## 6. Talking to the athlete
Don't moralize. "Resting now is the training" is true. Avoid streak and make-up language. If they push to train through: respect autonomy, state the risk plainly once (a longer illness, rarely a heart problem), offer a lower-stakes alternative (walk, easy mobility), and agree on the stop rule ("if you have fever, chest symptoms or feel worse, stop and rest").

## Worked examples
- **Wednesday sore throat and runny nose, feels fine, wants to run.** *"Above the neck, so a 25-minute easy jog is fine if it feels good. No tempo tomorrow; I'll swap it for easy and we'll see Friday."* Wake Thursday morning: how are they?
- **Thursday: fever 38.4, aches.** *"Rest. No running until you've been fever-free for a full day. I'm clearing this week's quality; we'll rebuild next week at ~60%."* Update plan; schedule a check-in.
- **Heavy squat day after a 3-day cold with a fever on day one.** Not the heavy day. Fever-free 24 h first, then a moderate session at ~75% of planned loads with two sets fewer; heavy work the following week if that felt normal.
- **Sunday long run (28 km) after a 4-day illness.** Not 28. First run easy 40 min on Saturday, long run ~14 to 16 km next Sunday, normal build the Sunday after.

## Pitfalls
Treating "fever-free" as "fully better". Reintroducing intensity first. Stacking missed workouts. Making the athlete feel they're losing everything. Missing red flags in a lingering illness. Ignoring that a recurring cold may signal under-fueling or overload.

## Evidence notes (citations from memory; verify before quoting)
- Neck check: *weak/expert opinion*. Moderate exercise during a mild upper-respiratory illness did not worsen symptoms in a small experimental study (Weidner et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 1998), which supports permitting easy exercise with above-the-neck symptoms (*weak/moderate*).
- No exercise with fever; myocarditis concern after viral illness with exertion: *expert consensus* from case reports and sports-cardiology statements (rare, but severe).
- COVID-19 graded return: *expert consensus* (published return-to-play guidance in sports-medicine journals, ~2020 to 2021); evolving.
- Detraining after short breaks: *moderate* (Mujika and Padilla, Sports Med 2000).
- Return percentages and timelines: *expert opinion*, conservative defaults.
